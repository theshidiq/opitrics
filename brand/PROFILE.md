# Account identity

Copy and asset spec for the public profiles. Assets are generated by
`brand/make_assets.py` into `brand/out/` — regenerate rather than
hand-editing, and re-run it if `remotion/src/theme.ts` changes, because
the palette is mirrored from there.

No bio or handle here is published. Registering handles and pasting
bios is the user's call, per profile, per platform.

One exception, and it is worth knowing about: the naming line on the
public sources index is generated from `scripts/deploy_sources.py`, so
that copy ships whenever the site is deployed. Change it there and
here together, or the two drift.

## Name

**Opitrics**, kept. It is a contraction of **opinion metrics**, which
is what the pipeline does: it turns opinions into measurements.

The derivation settles the two things that would otherwise be wrong
with the name. Pronunciation is **oh-PIT-rics** — not an arbitrary
choice to dodge the "tricks" reading, but what *opinion* (oh-PIN-yun)
already implies, which is why it will stick. And the name is not
meaningless after all; it just needs saying once.

So wherever there is room for a line of text, say the derivation rather
than inventing a second concept: **Opitrics — opinion metrics**.

One limit on that phrase: it cannot stand alone as a bio. "Opinion
metrics" sounds like market research, market research implies buyers,
and commenters are not buyers. It works beside the name as an
etymology; the bios below do the actual explaining and carry the
caveat.

A rename was considered and rejected. The wordmark is burned into the
bottom-left safe area of every rendered short
(`remotion/src/components.tsx:85`), so a rename means a window where
the watermark and the account name disagree — on an account whose
product is "what you see matches what's true", that is a self-inflicted
wound in the wrong place.

### Where the derivation is said, and where it is not

Settled 2026-08-25. This file is the source; the rest defer to it.

It appears in `README.md`, `CLAUDE.md`, both agent briefs
(`social-strategist`, `web-steward`), the web dashboard masthead, the
public sources index footer, `.env.example` and `scripts/doctor.py`.
The caveat above travels with it in every one of those — a phrase that
needs a warning is not safe to copy without the warning.

Two places state it more than in passing, for the same reason. The
public sources index is where a skeptic lands, so the caveat is content
rather than a rule: *"It reads what people say and counts it. It does
not survey buyers or measure sales, and a comment section is not a
sample of either."* And `web-steward` gets the strongest version,
because a page implying this is buyer research would undercut the one
claim the site exists to make.

**Not on the video watermark.** Same reasoning that rejected the
rename: the wordmark is burned into every rendered short, so changing
it splits old files from new. A strapline also has nowhere to sit on a
1080px frame beside the safe-area rules. The watermark stays bare
`OPITRICS`.

**Not in the per-subject audit page footers.** Those already carry the
subject, comment and video counts, fetch date, generation date and the
withheld-names note. The index page a visitor passes through carries
the derivation instead; repeating it on every subject page is noise on
the pages that most need to read as evidence rather than as branding.

**Not in Japanese.** See the Japanese arm below — the mechanism that
earns it in English does not exist there.

## The Japanese arm

Not built yet, and deliberately not launched with the English one. The
copy below is ready so the handles can be held and the identity is
settled before the work starts.

**It is a different publication, not a translation.** On the English
side the user is partly blind — trusting the pipeline's translation
checks, unable to fully judge whether a hook lands. On the Japanese
side they read the raw comment, feel the register, and catch the
sarcasm the model flagged as `nuance`. That asymmetry is large enough
that the Japanese arm is plausibly the better product, and treating it
as phase two of the English one would quietly shape it into a dub.

In practice that means different subjects (Japanese-market cars with
natively Japanese comment sections), a different angle set — `fight` is
built around nationality arguments and paid-shill accusations, which is
an English-language-YouTube pathology, and should be cut — and hooks
built differently, because the flat confrontational opener that works
in English short-form reads as crude rather than arresting in Japanese.

**The biggest risk is a dub that passes every gate.** `check_quotes()`
passes, `opitrics/render/translate.py` is never invoked, no tofu,
nothing wrong on any frame — and the video is lifeless. The failure is
not factual, so the automated layer cannot structurally catch it; only
retention would show it, after publication. The guard is not
discipline, it is choosing a launch subject the English arm has never
covered, so there is no original to translate from.

### Handles

Hold `opitrics_jp` and `opitricsjp` on all four now. The underscore
breaks the read-aloud property that justified the primary handle, so
`opitricsjp` is the more consistent choice — but hold both and decide
later. Registering costs nothing; losing the name is unrecoverable.

### Display name

`Opitrics｜コメント欄を数える` — 18 characters, inside TikTok's and
Instagram's 30. YouTube stays bare `Opitrics`.

The full-width `｜` is deliberate; an ASCII pipe looks wrong beside
Japanese.

**The "opinion metrics" derivation is dropped in Japanese.** It earns
its place in English because *opinion* (oh-PIN-yun) makes the
pronunciation stick. That mechanism does not exist in Japanese, so the
phrase would carry the market-research liability noted above with none
of the benefit. `オピトリクス` in the About text fixes pronunciation
directly if it needs fixing.

This is the one place the English propagation does not follow. Anything
generated for a Japanese surface — a sources index in Japanese, a
Japanese dashboard — takes the display name and the bios below, not a
translated etymology. 「オピニオン・メトリクス」 would read as a
loanword gloss of a word Japanese already has, and 市場調査 is exactly
the misreading the caveat exists to prevent.

The fourth rule is the Japanese-only-quotes rule, published. The
English channel has no equivalent because English content has verified
inbound translation; Japanese content has the restriction instead.
Publishing a rule you actually keep is the cheapest credibility
available, and it answers the obvious "why no English comments?"
as rigour rather than as a gap.

### Before the first Japanese short ships

Editorial, and cheap enough to do now: read several Japanese-native
corpora raw and confirm or falsify the claims above about angle fit
before building on them; pick a launch subject; fix the angle set;
rewrite the narration brief for Japanese hook construction rather than
translating the English one.

Pipeline, and handed back — none of it is this file's to do:
`remotion/src/theme.ts` needs a simplified-CJK face mirroring
`_cjk_font()` in `opitrics/render/cards.py`; `strip_emoji` re-verified against a
full-Japanese string; font cmap checked for every glyph on every card
rather than quote bodies only; a Japanese ElevenLabs voice chosen by
measurement the way `DEFAULT_VOICE` was; `check_quotes()` confirmed to
work unmodified; and a rendered short inspected frame by frame.

Those are the traps `CLAUDE.md` already documents. All-Japanese output
takes each of them from latent to certain. A Japanese launch with tofu
in a title is worse than no Japanese launch.

## About / pinned post

Runs as the X pinned post, the TikTok and Instagram pinned comment, and
the top of the YouTube About tab. 121 words.

```
Every video here starts the same way: pull the comments under a car's
videos, in every language they were written in, group them by what
people are actually arguing about, and count each group.

Then the part that matters. Every figure on screen has a permalink
behind it. Open it, read the comment, check my count. If a number is
wrong, that is a real error and I want to know.

Some things this can't tell you. People who comment are not people who
bought. Nothing here is a sales signal or a review. The loudest group
in a comment section is usually not the majority, and I only call it
one when it is.

No name on this, no face. Just the count and the receipts.

(Opitrics — opinion metrics. oh-PIT-rics.)
```

Three things hold this together:

- **Anonymity appears once**, eleven words, second-to-last line, as a
  housekeeping fact. It is never argued for. Anything longer converts it
  from a fact into a topic. Never write a line whose job is to justify
  being anonymous.
- **"If a number is wrong, that is a real error and I want to know"** is
  the highest-value sentence in the package. A falsifiable commitment is
  the only credibility available without a name, and it recruits hostile
  readers into fact-checking for free.
- **The limits paragraph sits before the sign-off, not buried.**
  Volunteering what the method cannot do is the fastest legible proxy
  for honesty, and it inoculates against the "comment sentiment equals
  sales" misread that would otherwise appear under every video.

## Voice

**First person singular.** A plural entity voice implies staff, which is
a quiet lie in a project built on not overstating — and an anonymous
*entity* is a faceless machine, while an anonymous *person* is a source.
That difference is entirely grammatical and it is the whole persona.

The discipline that makes it survive contact: **the "I" attaches to a
decision, never to a feat.** "I sorted these into nine groups." "I
called this one contested because the replies split." "I didn't run this
angle — the data was thin." Never "I read 16,000 comments" — a machine
embedded and clustered; a human read cluster summaries and made calls.
The moment you claim the machine's labour, the AI-slop accusation
becomes true and there is no defence. Claim the judgment, credit the
machine with the volume.

Verbs that survive scrutiny: *sorted*, *pulled*, *went through*,
*counted*. Not *read*.

### Replying to "this is just AI slop / you didn't read anything"

```
Fair — and no, I didn't read 16,000 comments. A machine grouped them
and counted; I named the groups and decided what was worth a video.
That's the honest division of labour.

Which is why every number is a link. Card 3 says 1,412 comments about
the rear seat. Open it, count a few, tell me if I'm off. Nobody
auditing a real analyst gets that.
```

It concedes the true part in the first four words, because the
accusation is half right and pretending otherwise loses the lurkers —
who are the actual audience for any reply. It then draws the line the
persona depends on, and ends by handing over a task small enough to
actually do.

**One rule for replies generally:** never reply to a hostile comment
without a number in it. If you cannot cite a specific figure and its
link, do not engage — a reply without a receipt is just an argument, and
arguments are the one thing this account has no advantage in.

## Assets

Generated by `brand/make_assets.py`. The palette mirrors
`remotion/src/theme.ts`, not the warm review-page CSS in
`scripts/upload.py` — the shorts are white-ground, black-text,
`#0546A8` accent, and that is the palette a viewer actually meets.

| File | Use |
|---|---|
| `avatar-800.png` | Primary avatar, all platforms |
| `avatar-180.png` | Same mark, pre-sized |
| `avatar-800-small.png` | Reply-size variant — see below |
| `explore/contact-sheet.png` | Rejected candidates, for the record |
| `avatar-wordmark-800.png` | Typographic fallback, `OP` |
| `banner-2560x1440.png` | YouTube channel art |
| `banner-crop-*.png` | The crops YouTube shows, for checking |
| `proof-avatar-sizes.png` | Avatar at 88/48/24px on both grounds |

**The mark** is two bars, each divided at a different point — white
against `#0546A8`. Ink ground: inverted from the shorts so the avatar
reads as an object on a white feed rather than a hole in it.

It means the finding, not the method. What this account produces is
almost never "people liked it" — it is that the comment section split,
and the interesting number is where. Neither division sits near the
middle, deliberately: a comment section that divides evenly is the one
result the data essentially never gives, and a centred mark would claim
it. Two bars rather than one for a reason that is not aesthetic — a
single divided bar reads as a progress bar or a battery, and that
association is strong enough to overwrite the meaning. Nothing displays
two progress bars at different values, so the second bar destroys the
misreading.

It is not a car. A car silhouette promises reviews, attracts an audience
the first short disappoints, and looks like every other automotive
channel at thumbnail size.

**Two avatar variants.** The small one thickens the bars, tightens the
notch and widens the inset, because at 24px the divisions are the first
thing to close up. Check `proof-avatar-sizes.png` before uploading; the
800px master proves nothing. At 24px the mark holds as two blue-and-white
bars but the split points are gone — that is the honest floor.

**The banner** puts all text inside the 1546×423 mobile-safe centre.
A field of divided bars — the same mark, many findings each splitting
its own way — extends left and right, fading fast above and below so it
never turns the wordmark into something read through a screen door. On
mobile you see none of it; on desktop the edges; on TV the whole thing.
The mobile crop reads complete rather than truncated, and the wider
crops are a reward. The lower-left of the desktop strip is deliberately
empty — the avatar overlaps there.

No subject car in the banner: it would date the channel to one video
and imply the channel is about that car.

## Open question, partly resolved

The bio promises every number links to its source. The question was
whether that promise should appear *on screen* rather than only in the
description and the audit pages, since a viewer who never taps through
would otherwise see the watermark and nothing else.

Half of it is now answered in code. Every angle ends on a `sources`
card reading "every quote links to the original comment" under a
`check the work` eyebrow (`opitrics/render/sequence.py:243`), so the
promise is on screen in all five shorts.

What is still open is the URL. `scripts/shorts.py --sources-url` bakes
a specific address into that card's narration, and it is currently
unset, so the card makes the promise without saying where to go. That
is not a branding decision — it waits on the sources site being live at
a stable address, which is `web-steward`'s call. Until then the shorts
should not name a URL that might 404: a dead link under "check the
work" is worse than no link at all, on the one card whose entire job is
to be checkable.
